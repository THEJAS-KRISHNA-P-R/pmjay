# Platform Conventions

Apple's core principles are shared across platforms, but the actual controls, layout patterns, and concrete sizing are not — a good iPad app isn't a stretched iPhone app, and a good Mac app isn't a windowed iPad app. Check this before designing or reviewing anything platform-specific — using the wrong control for the platform is a bigger miss than getting its styling slightly off, and shipping the wrong numeric spec (a touch target, a bar height) is as real a miss as either.

## iOS (iPhone)

- **Safe areas are non-negotiable.** Respect the notch/Dynamic Island and the home indicator — content shouldn't be obscured by them, but full-bleed backgrounds (color, imagery, blur) can and often should extend into them.
- **44×44pt is the minimum tap target for every interactive element**, regardless of how small its visible glyph is — pad the hit area rather than shrinking the target. This number comes from actual human-factors research on fingertip contact area, not an arbitrary round figure, which is exactly why it isn't a value to compromise on when a design gets visually crowded.
- **Tab bars for top-level navigation, 3–5 destinations** (a 6th and beyond collapses into a "More" list, which costs an extra tap and is a worse experience, not a clever escape hatch), **navigation stacks with a back gesture** for drilling into detail. Don't build custom back-navigation that fights the system swipe-back gesture. Tab bars stay visible and enabled throughout a tab's navigation stack — hiding or disabling tabs based on context disorients people, since it changes the one part of the screen that's supposed to be stable.
- **Even Apple's own tab bar layout isn't fixed forever — treat current specifics as current, not eternal.** iOS 26 moved search out of the tab bar into an isolated floating button; iOS 27 (2026) reversed that and reintegrated it. If you're matching current system conventions closely, it's worth confirming what the *current* OS version actually does rather than assuming a screenshot from a year ago is still accurate.
- **Sheets and modals** for focused, temporary tasks — use a partial-height sheet (people can still see context behind it) when the task doesn't need full attention, a full-screen modal when it does.
- **SF Symbols** for iconography — they're optically matched to San Francisco (the system typeface) at every weight and size, and support Dynamic Type scaling automatically. Custom icons that don't account for this tend to look visually inconsistent next to system UI. See `materials-typography.md` for weights, scales, and rendering modes.
- **Haptics are cheap to add and easy to overuse.** Reserve them for meaningful state changes (a toggle committing, an item snapping into place, a success/error moment).

## iPadOS

- **Design for multiple layout classes, not one fixed width.** The same view needs to work in a full-screen window, Split View, and Slide Over. Avoid layouts that assume a single fixed size.
- **Sidebars over tab bars once you have the space.** iPad has room to show navigation persistently — a collapsible sidebar (like Mail or Files) is usually a better fit than a tab bar once a screen has more than a handful of top-level sections.
- **Support pointer and keyboard as first-class input**, not as an afterthought bolted onto a touch-first design — hover states, keyboard shortcuts, and drag-and-drop between apps are expected on iPad in a way they aren't on iPhone.
- **Bar heights run larger than iPhone's** (navigation bars and toolbars are taller on iPad) — don't reuse iPhone constants verbatim if you're hand-rolling a web equivalent that needs to feel native at iPad-scale.

## macOS

- **The menu bar is the source of truth for every command**, even ones that also have a toolbar button or keyboard shortcut — if an action can't be found there, power users will assume it doesn't exist.
- **Traffic-light window controls live top-left**; don't relocate or restyle them without a strong reason, since their position is one of the most deeply ingrained spatial expectations on the platform.
- **Window corners are softer and larger than they used to be, and relate to what's docked to them.** A window with a toolbar uses a corner radius sized to wrap concentrically around that toolbar's glass elements — see "Concentricity" in `materials-typography.md` for the underlying math. Don't pick a window/panel corner radius in isolation from what's nested against it.
- **Sidebar + content + optional inspector is the default app shape** (Mail, Notes, Finder, Xcode). Reach for it before inventing a custom navigation pattern.
- **Keyboard-driven workflows matter more here than on touch platforms.** Every primary action should have a discoverable keyboard shortcut, and multi-step workflows benefit from command-palette-style search (⌘K-style) where practical.
- **Windows are resizable and often small.** Don't design a layout that only looks right at one specific window size.

## watchOS

- **Glanceable, not exhaustive.** A Watch screen answers one question or offers one action — if a flow needs more than a couple of taps, it probably belongs on the phone instead.
- **The Digital Crown is a precision input** for anything that needs fine control (scrolling a list, zooming, adjusting a value) — don't make people swipe with a fingertip on a tiny screen for tasks the Crown handles better. Scrolling the Crown from the watch face also opens the **Smart Stack** — a widget stack that, since watchOS 26, can surface proactive "hints" (a suggested action rendered as a glass-like prompt, triggered by context — location, routine, sensor data) rather than waiting to be asked.
- **Complications and notifications are often the actual product** — for a lot of Watch use cases, the glance *is* the interaction, not a shortcut into a bigger app.
- **watchOS 26 introduced a wrist-flick gesture** (tilt the wrist away) for one-handed dismissal — clearing a notification, muting a call, silencing a timer — specifically for when the other hand is occupied. Worth knowing as a real, current example of the platform designing for partial/occupied attention, not just full-focus interaction.
- **Liquid Glass reached the Watch too, but weigh that as a genuine trade-off, not an automatic win.** It shows up in notifications, Smart Stack, Control Center, and some in-app controls. But credible, watch-focused reviewers (Six Colors, among others) have made a fair point worth carrying into your own judgment: Liquid Glass's stated purpose is to get chrome out of the way and put focus on content — and on a screen this small, where UI rarely overlays real content the way it does over a photo or video on iPhone, that justification is weaker. Reviewers have also called the rollout visually uneven — prominent in some system surfaces, barely noticeable in others. Don't reach for glass on a watch face or complication just because it's the current aesthetic; ask whether it's actually serving the "content over chrome" goal it's supposed to, in that specific spot.

## visionOS

- **Depth is a layout axis, not just decoration.** Content can live at different distances from the person, and closer/farther placement itself communicates hierarchy and focus — use it deliberately rather than defaulting to everything on one plane.
- **Windows are ornamented, not chrome-heavy.** Controls (close, resize, window bar) live outside the main content window as "ornaments" so the content itself stays clean. 3D content that isn't a flat window uses a **volume** instead.
- **Design for where someone is looking and reaching, not just what's on a flat canvas** — gaze + pinch replaces cursor + click, and hit targets need to account for that instead of assuming pixel-precise pointing.
- **visionOS 26 shifted the platform's default mental model from episodic to persistent.** Spatial widgets — for Photos, Music, Weather, and others — can be pinned to a physical surface (or floated freely), stay exactly where placed across a full restart, and are only visible to people actually in the same room as the placement. That's a meaningfully different relationship to software than "open it, use it, it goes away" — if you're designing anything for this platform, decide deliberately whether it should behave like a persistent object in someone's space or a traditional app that appears and dismisses.
- **When something is spatially persistent, moving it has to move everything associated with it.** If a person repositions an object, its controls and related content need to follow — a mismatch between an object's position and its controls' position is what makes a spatial interface feel broken rather than solid, since people's spatial expectations are doing real predictive work about where things "should" be.
- **This is genuinely the lowest-priority platform for a web/React stack** — but one specific capability is worth knowing regardless: Safari's spatial browsing mode can render embedded 3D models and depth-enhanced "spatial scenes" directly inline in an ordinary web page, no native app required. If a product ever calls for an in-browser 3D product view, this is the relevant current entry point rather than a fully custom WebGL build.

## CarPlay

Included for completeness, with an honest flag: this is the one platform here that's categorically inapplicable to most products, not just a low priority for a particular tech stack. Apple restricts CarPlay apps to a small, fixed set of categories — audio, navigation, communication, EV charging/fueling, quick food ordering, and public-safety dispatch. A general SaaS product, an e-commerce storefront, or a WhatsApp-style CRM simply **isn't eligible to be a CarPlay app at all**, regardless of how well it's built.

For the rare case it does apply: CarPlay is **template-only** — `CPListTemplate`, `CPGridTemplate`, and similar system-provided templates, populated with your content. There is no custom UI, no custom layout, no custom animation; iOS renders everything, which is also why there's no responsive-design work to do across the wide range of in-car display sizes. The design work that remains is entirely about content: keep the most important information and controls in the upper half of the screen, use a limited color palette that doesn't reuse the same color for interactive and non-interactive elements, and remember the platform's one hard rule — the driver's iPhone should not need to be touched, unlocked, or accessible at all while CarPlay is active. Notably, **Live Activities can appear on the CarPlay Dashboard even for apps that aren't CarPlay apps themselves** — so a delivery-tracking or ride-status Live Activity, the kind of thing a normal product might actually build, reaches CarPlay without needing to go through this whole system at all.

## Settings and preferences screens

- **The grouped/inset list is the pattern, not a stylistic choice.** Apple's own Settings app is the reference: grouped sections, consistent typography, nothing competing for attention. A settings screen that piles toggles, disclosure chevrons, and promotional banners into one undifferentiated list is the thing to avoid — the grouping itself is what makes it scannable.
- **Row type communicates behavior — use the right one:** text with a trailing chevron means "navigate to a sub-screen to choose this"; a checkmark means single-select within a group (not a multi-select checklist the way a web checkbox list often implies); a switch means an immediate on/off toggle with no further screen; red text is reserved specifically for destructive actions (turn off, delete, remove) — don't use red for anything else, since people learn to read it as a warning.
- **Not every setting belongs in the same place.** iOS lets an app surface some preferences directly in the system Settings app via a Settings bundle — appropriate for things people configure rarely and don't need quick access to. Anything people adjust often belongs in-app, where it's actually reachable.
- **On macOS, preferences are domain-scoped** — system-level and app-level preferences are kept distinguishable, and an app's own preferences can override system defaults for that app specifically, rather than everything living in one flat namespace.

## Shared conventions worth double-checking

- **Dark mode is a first-class palette, not an inverted light mode**, and needs *more* separation between background levels, not less. Use semantic colors (label, secondaryLabel, systemBackground, etc.) that Apple's platforms already remap per-appearance, rather than hardcoding light-mode colors and flipping them — see "Color" in `materials-typography.md`.
- **Dynamic Type / text-size scaling should be assumed**, not treated as an edge case — build layouts that reflow gracefully as text grows, especially on iOS and iPadOS. The actual size scale, its non-uniform per-style behavior, and the bugs it exposes are in `accessibility-feedback.md`.
- **Accessibility (VoiceOver, Switch Control, Reduce Motion, Reduce Transparency, right-to-left layout) is part of "done," not a pass you do at the end** — see `accessibility-feedback.md`.
- **When you need the exact current spec rather than an approximation** (a component's precise padding, a system color, a current icon), Apple publishes official Figma/Sketch kits and the SF font families at developer.apple.com/design/resources — closer to ground truth than eyeballing a screenshot, and updated alongside each OS release.
